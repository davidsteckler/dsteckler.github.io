Part 2: The Lucky Break

You decoded the message. Now figure out what one of its strangest lines actually means.

FROM THE TRANSMISSION
A LOT OF FUTURES START WITH ONE LUCKY BREAK.

What does that mean?

In the late 1960s, almost no kid your age had ever touched a computer.

Bill Gates did.

Not because he invented one. Not because he was already famous. His school, Lakeside in Seattle, happened to put a computer terminal close enough for students to use.

That was the lucky break.

A Teletype ASR-33. No screen. Programs and answers came through paper.

The terminal did not look like the computers you know. It looked more like a typewriter. The real computer was somewhere else, reached through a phone line.

Computer time was expensive. Students had limited access. Mistakes could waste minutes that cost real money.

So Gates and the other students watched closely. They learned the machine. They tried things. They found mistakes. They came back again.

The advantage was access.

He was near something rare before it was obvious how important it would become.

Access did not guarantee Microsoft. The terminal did not magically make Gates successful.

He could have ignored it. He could have decided it was too confusing. He could have treated it like some strange school machine and moved on.

But he did not.

He moved closer.

He kept using it. He kept learning what the computer accepted and what it rejected. He learned that the machine did not care what he meant. It cared what he actually typed.

Years later, Gates described Lakeside's access as so important that he said:

“If there had been no Lakeside, there would have been no Microsoft.”
Bill Gates, speaking at Lakeside School, 2005

That is the part of the first message that matters.

A lucky break can put something valuable near you.

It still matters whether you notice it.

Your version may not be a rare computer terminal. It could be a class, a teacher, a tool, a project, a question, a skill, or some strange assignment that does not seem important yet.

You may not know which opportunities matter until much later.

But it is hard to use an opportunity you never notice.
