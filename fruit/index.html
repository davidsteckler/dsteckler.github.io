WHY DO TECH COMPANIES LOVE FRUIT?

Apple. Apricot. BlackBerry. Acorn. Tangerine. Raspberry Pi. Once you notice it, old computer history starts looking less like an electronics lab and more like a grocery aisle.

The short answer

There was never one secret fruit rule.

The names came from different stories. What ties them together is that fruit words are familiar, visual, friendly, short, memorable, and wonderfully unlike the cold technical names that filled early computing.

Friendly

“Apple” made a computer sound like something that belonged in a home.

Memorable

A normal word from the wrong category sticks in your brain.

Visual

You instantly get a logo, shape, color, and personality.

A tradition

Eventually, new computer makers were consciously joining an old naming joke.

The best evidence that this became a real tradition: Raspberry Pi founder Eben Upton explicitly said “Raspberry” was a reference to the old microcomputer habit of naming companies after fruit.
The fruit bowl of computing

Every name has its own weird little story.

There is no single origin myth that explains all of them. The fun part is watching a useful branding idea slowly turn into computer culture.

🍎
1976 · Apple Computer

APPLE

Steve Jobs had been spending time around an apple farm in Oregon. He later said the word sounded “fun, spirited, and not intimidating.” Steve Wozniak also remembered that the name helped suggest a computer that ordinary people could understand and keep at home.

That last part matters. In the 1970s, a name like Apple did not sound like a machine room, engineering department, or military contractor.
SOURCE: JOBS' EXPLANATION ↗
🌰
1978 · Acorn Computers

ACORN

Acorn co-founder Hermann Hauser later recalled that the team knew about Apple and actually kicked around other fruit names. “Acorn” won because of the English saying about mighty oaks growing from little acorns, and because it made a clean visual symbol.

Yes: an acorn is botanically a fruit. This makes the fruit-computer rabbit hole even better.
SOURCE: COMPUTER HISTORY MUSEUM ORAL HISTORY ↗
🍑
1980s · Apricot Computers

APRICOT

British computer maker ACT began selling machines under the Apricot name in the early 1980s and later became Apricot Computers. What matters for this story is that Raspberry Pi founder Eben Upton specifically points to Apricot as one of the old fruit-named computer companies he remembered.

By the 1980s, “fruit + computer” no longer looked like a one-off accident.
SOURCE: EBEN UPTON INTERVIEW ↗
🫐
1998 · Research In Motion

BLACKBERRY

This one was deliberate branding. Naming firm Lexicon wanted a word that felt more natural and pleasant than names like EasyMail or ProMail. The device's tiny keyboard buttons also resembled the little drupelets on a blackberry.

The name worked partly because it was unexpected. A fruit name made an email machine feel less stressful and much easier to remember.
SOURCE: THE FIRM THAT NAMED BLACKBERRY ↗
🍊
1979 · Tangerine Computer Systems

TANGERINE

Tangerine Computer Systems was another British microcomputer company from the same era. Decades later, Upton would name-check Tangerine alongside Apricot and Acorn when explaining the fruit-computer tradition behind Raspberry Pi.

At this point the British microcomputer scene was basically assembling a fruit salad.
SOURCE: EBEN UPTON INTERVIEW ↗
🍓
2012 · Raspberry Pi

RASPBERRY PI

This is the smoking gun. Eben Upton said “Raspberry” was chosen as a direct reference to the fruit-naming tradition of old microcomputers. “Pi” came from Python, because an early version of the idea centered heavily on running Python.

A joke/tradition from the late 1970s was still alive more than thirty years later.
SOURCE: EBEN UPTON INTERVIEW ↗
🌰
Tiny botanical plot twist

ACORN COUNTS.

An acorn is the fruit of an oak tree. So the classic Apple → Acorn comparison is even more literally fruity than it looks.

Things that sound true but need a little cleanup

So was Apple the reason for ALL of this?

“Everyone copied Apple.”

Too simple. Acorn's founders explicitly remembered Apple while choosing their name, and Raspberry Pi explicitly references the older tradition. BlackBerry arrived through a separate professional naming process.

“Fruit names were random.”

Not really. Jobs liked that Apple felt fun and non-intimidating. BlackBerry's namers liked the natural associations and memorability. These are useful qualities for technology meant for ordinary people.

“It was all marketing.”

Sometimes. BlackBerry absolutely was a branding exercise. Apple began with a much more personal story. Acorn mixed marketing, symbolism, and a founder's idea.

“Raspberry Pi was just a cute name.”

It was also a history joke. Upton directly connected the name to Tangerine, Apricot, and Acorn. The old microcomputer fruit bowl was intentional.
