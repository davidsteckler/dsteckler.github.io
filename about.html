// about.david

I wasn’t the kid who found school easy.

I’m David Steckler, a 7th grade computer science teacher at Highlands Middle School in White Plains, New York. I teach coding, robotics, problem solving, and the kind of thinking that helps students become less helpless in the modern world.

I had to fight for understanding myself. That turned out to matter. It made me pay attention to what finally makes an idea click, why people give up, and what changes when they start trusting that they can figure something out.

What’s obvious to you is amazing to others. This works both ways. What I take for granted blows my students’ minds. What they shrug off, I know is worth something.

// how I got here

The path made more sense looking backward.

I wasn’t the kid gliding through school. I had to work for understanding, and I remember what it feels like when an explanation almost makes sense but still doesn’t land. That experience became useful later because it made me care about clarity, patience, and the exact moment confusion turns into understanding.

Early yearsI was curious, but school did not always feel natural. I learned a lot through trial, error, and having to find my own way into ideas.
Teaching tennisAs a teenager at a Nike tennis camp, I got my first taste of helping someone improve at something and actually feel the improvement. That hooked me.
Computer scienceI found a subject where thinking becomes visible. A line of code produces a result. A wrong assumption shows itself. An idea becomes something you can test.
NowI teach 7th grade computer science and robotics, build curriculum and tools, and keep trying to make school feel more alive, more honest, and more connected to the world students are entering.
// why I teach

I like watching something click for someone who thought they couldn’t do it.

Most of school trains kids to chase grades, comply, and move on. I hated that then. I still hate it. The kind of teaching I care about feels different. More alive. More honest. Built around real understanding, not performance.

Middle school matters because students are old enough to think in interesting ways, while their habits, identity, and relationship with challenge are still changing quickly. This is where a lot of people quietly decide what kind of learner they think they are.

I want students to leave my room with more confidence in their ability to figure things out. More tolerance for mistakes. More respect for their own mind. More willingness to stay with a hard problem instead of ejecting the second it gets uncomfortable.

There’s no speed limit on learning. The real danger is deciding too early what kind of person you are, and then living inside that sentence forever.
// a belief that shapes how I teach and how I keep learning
// how the room works

Try. Fail. Notice what happened. Adjust. Go again.

That loop drives most of what I do. Video games figured this out decades ago. School mostly hasn’t. Kids stay engaged when they can act, get feedback, revise, and see progress in real time.

Code is especially useful because the feedback is immediate and honest. The computer does exactly what you told it, not what you meant. That makes it a great place to practice precision, patience, sequencing, debugging, and thinking more carefully.

Immediate feedback

Students test ideas in real time, spot mistakes quickly, and build confidence through revision.

Student ownership

I want students making decisions and explaining why, not just following steps and clicking what they were told.

Useful struggle

I do not remove every obstacle. Productive frustration matters. Confusion is normal and survivable.

  • Hands-on work instead of long lectures.
  • One clear idea at a time, with room to test it.
  • Projects that feel real instead of fake-school busywork.
  • Students talking, explaining ideas to each other, and learning that being useful to other people is a real skill.
  • Space for actual questions and real conversation.
  • Progress that students can see and feel.
// why computer science

The code is real. The deeper subject is judgment.

Computer science is not just about learning a programming language. It is a way of learning how modern systems work, how instructions become outcomes, how information is represented, and how small choices can produce huge downstream effects.

When students learn to code, they are also learning precision, sequencing, pattern recognition, debugging, and patience. They are learning how to break a problem into parts. They are learning that vague thinking creates vague results. If they want a machine to do something useful, they have to become clearer themselves.

That is why I take representation seriously. A picture on a screen, a sound in a speaker, a sentence in a chatbot, a game, a website, a robot movement — all of it comes back to information, structure, and logic. Once students really feel that, the world starts to look different.

I also care about the layer underneath the code: identity, assumptions, what knowing actually means, how to hold a belief without becoming its prisoner, ethics, systems, choices, logic, and the gap between what technology can do and what people should do with it.

Coding is valuable. The bigger win is becoming someone who can understand systems, adapt quickly, and create instead of only consume.
David Steckler
// AI and new tools

AI belongs in school, but access is not enough.

AI is already changing how people write, search, plan, analyze, and create. Students need experience using these tools, but they also need judgment. They need to know when AI is helping, when it is flattening their thinking, and when they are outsourcing something they should still know how to do themselves.

I am interested in teaching students how to use AI as leverage, not as a crutch. That means prompt design, verification, revision, and knowing that a confident answer is not the same thing as a correct answer. Their own brain still matters. A lot.

AI as amplifier

Brainstorm faster. Test ideas faster. Explain concepts in multiple ways. Move faster from a blank page to meaningful work.

AI with skepticism

Question outputs, compare sources, notice weak reasoning, and understand that fluent language can still hide errors.

// what students and I build

I want ideas to turn into things you can point to.

I want students creating things they can point to, not completing invisible exercises and moving on. Depending on the unit, that can mean code, graphics, animations, logic puzzles, games, web pages, robotics challenges, collaborative problem solving, or AI-driven projects that make students think harder about the process behind the result.

I am especially drawn to work that bridges abstract ideas and concrete results. A line of code becomes a shape. A variable changes a pattern. A sequence becomes behavior. An idea becomes something real.

Most of my own work right now sits at the intersection of teaching, AI, entrepreneurship, and building better experiences for students. I want class to be cleaner, smarter, more interactive, and more honest about what actually helps people learn.

When my students leave, I want them to have more than a grade. I want them to have proof to themselves that they can make things, solve problems, and build something they respect.

// what drives me now

Make learning feel more real.

I care about making learning feel more modern and more connected to the world students are actually entering. I want to keep building classrooms and digital tools that respect students’ intelligence while helping them become more capable, more articulate, and more self-directed.

The long game is simple: help young people become harder to fool, harder to discourage, and more able to build a life with intention.

Students do not need another adult who only tells them what to finish. They need adults who help them see further, think better, and trust that they can learn things that once felt out of reach.

That is the kind of classroom I’m trying to build.
